# Sources and credits

No rule in this corpus is original. Every one restates, in a form an agent can
apply, advice that editors and linguists worked out over the last century. The
wording here is ours; the thinking is theirs.

**What was taken and what was not.** Ideas, principles, and tests are not
copyrightable and are restated in our own words throughout. No passage from any
book below is reproduced. Where an openly licensed guide contributed, the licence
is named and the attribution requirement is met here and in `NOTICE`.

## Books behind the clarity rules

- **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.** The backbone of
  `C1`–`C4` and `C8`–`C10`: characters as subjects, actions as verbs,
  nominalizations, old information before new, topic strings, stress position. If
  you read one book on this list, read this one.
- **Richard A. Lanham, *Revising Prose*.** The paramedic method in `C5`, and the
  diagnosis of what he called the Official Style.
- **George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing"
  (*American Scientist*, 1990).** Reader-expectation theory: `C8`, `C9`, `C15`.
- **Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style*.** The curse of knowledge, classic style,
  and the case against treating every passive and every long word as a fault:
  `C6`, `C19`.
- **William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*.** Clutter, `C17`, `C21`.
- **Bryan A. Garner, *Garner's Modern English Usage*.** The usage judgments behind
  much of `U11`–`U18`.
- **William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (1918).** Public domain in the
  United States. The ancestor of `C21` and the redundancy list in `U16`.
- **H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English* (1908).** Public domain. The
  short-word preference in `C19`.
- **Ernest Gowers, *Plain Words* (1948).** The plain-English tradition that
  `plainlanguage.gov` later codified.
- **George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946).** Dead metaphors and
  the operator-verb pattern: `U17`, `C4`. Still under copyright in the United
  States; nothing is quoted here.
- **Robert W. Bly, *The Copywriter's Handbook*, and John Caples, *Tested
  Advertising Methods*.** Reserved for the marketing module planned for v2.

## Openly licensed guides

These contributed conventions rather than prose, and their licences permit reuse
with attribution:

- **Federal Plain Language Guidelines** and **plainlanguage.gov**, by the Plain

  Attribution is requested and given.
- **18F Content Guide**, GSA. Public domain (US Government work).
- **Google developer documentation style guide.** Licensed CC BY 4.0. Contributed
  conventions in `U19`–`U28`.
- **GOV.UK style guide**, Government Digital Service. Open Government Licence v3.0.

## Prior art in tooling

Worth knowing about, and worth using alongside this corpus:

- **proselint** (BSD): a prose linter whose modules encode advice from Garner,
  Pinker, Orwell, and others. Deterministic where this corpus is judgmental.
- **Vale** (MIT): a configurable prose linter with packages implementing the
  Microsoft and Google style guides.
- **write-good** and **alex** (MIT): narrow linters for weak constructions and for
  insensitive wording.
- **hardikpandya/stop-slop**: the skill that made "AI tells" a category people
  actively edit for.
- **talkstream/ru-text**: the same idea for Russian, and the reason this project
  exists. `en-text` shares no code or text with it.

## What we are not

This is not a grammar checker, not a fact checker, and not a plagiarism detector. It
has no opinion about whether a text was written by a machine, only about whether it
reads like one. Those are different questions. A human can write slop; a model can
write well.
