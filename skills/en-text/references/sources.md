# Sources and credits

Where the rules come from, family by family. The wording throughout is ours.

Most clarity and usage rules restate advice from books on style, usage guides, and
style manuals, and each entry below names the rules that restate it. Two families
are the project's own: the slop rules (`S1`–`S24`) catalog patterns seen in model
output, and the method rules (`M1`–`M4`) came out of the project's reviews in
September 2026, as did `C23`. A few rules restate common editorial practice that we
did not trace to a single source, and a section below lists them as such.

**What was taken and what was not.** Ideas, principles, and tests are not
copyrightable and are restated in our own words throughout. No passage from any
book below is reproduced. Where an openly licensed guide contributed, the license is
named, and `NOTICE` gives the attribution each license asks for.

## Books on style and copyediting

- **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.** Later editions are
  co-authored, first with Gregory G. Colomb and then with Joseph Bizup (12th ed.,
  Pearson, 2016). The backbone of the clarity rules: characters as subjects and
  actions as verbs (`C1`, `C2`), nominalizations and the empty verbs that carry them
  (`C3`, `C4`), the subject early in the sentence (`C7`, `C16`), old information
  before new (`C8`), stress position (`C9`), topic strings (`C10`), metadiscourse
  (`C17`), and hedges (`C18`). His lesson on concision also supplies the redundant
  pairs in `U16`. Of the books listed here, this is the one to read first.
- **Richard A. Lanham, *Revising Prose*.** `C5` adapts his paramedic method, and the
  corpus takes from him the diagnosis of what he called the Official Style.
- **George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing"
  (*American Scientist*, 1990).** Reader-expectation theory: old before new (`C8`),
  the stress position (`C9`), and a verb that follows its subject closely (`C15`).
- **Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style*.** The curse of knowledge and classic style,
  as background, and the case that the passive often earns its place (`C6`).
- **Rebecca Johnson, Marine Corps University.** The "by zombies" test for spotting a
  passive (`C6`), which she posted in 2012.
- **William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*.** Clutter: `C17`, `C21`.
- **William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (1918).** Public domain in the
  United States. On composition: the emphatic words last (`C9`); a paragraph per
  topic, opened by its topic sentence (`C11`); parallel form for parallel ideas
  (`C13`); the subject near its verb (`C15`); specific, concrete language (`C20`);
  and no needless words (`C21`, `U16`). On usage: the serial comma (`U1`), the comma
  splice (`U2`), the possessive `'s` and its classical exceptions (`U6`), `fewer`
  against `less` (`U13`), and `affect` against `effect` (`U14`).
- **H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, *The King's English* (1906; 2nd ed. 1908).**
  Public domain. The preference for the short word (`C19`) and for the concrete word
  (`C20`).
- **Ernest Gowers, *Plain Words* (1948).** Plain English for official writing: the
  tradition the government guides below belong to.
- **George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946).** Worn-out, dying
  metaphors (`U17`); he counted a metaphor dead once it had become an ordinary word,
  and left those alone. Also the padded verb phrase that stands in for a plain verb
  (`C4`), and the short word over the long (`C19`). Still under copyright in the
  United States; nothing is quoted here.
- **Amy Einsohn and Marilyn Schwartz, *The Copyeditor's Handbook*, 4th ed.
  (University of California Press, 2019).** The tradition `C23` belongs to: the book
  counts consistency among the copyeditor's four chief concerns, and has the
  copyeditor query internal inconsistencies for the author to resolve. The rule
  itself came from this project's own review, described below.

## Usage guides and style manuals

- **Bryan A. Garner, *Garner's Modern English Usage*.** The usage judgments behind
  much of `U11`–`U18`, among them the restrictive `that` (`U11`) and `that` for
  people as good English (`U12`).
- **H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926).** The proposal
  behind `U11`: `that` for restrictive clauses and `which` for the rest. Fowler
  granted that neither most writers nor the best ones followed it.
- **Merriam-Webster.** Its usage notes treat `that` for people as standard (`U12`),
  and its dictionary gives `insure` the sense of making certain (`U14`).
- **The Chicago Manual of Style.** The closed-up em dash (`U5`) and the four dots of
  an omission that follows a complete sentence (`U10`).
- **The AP Stylebook.** The spaced em dash (`U5`), the same four dots (`U10`), `who`
  for people (`U12`), singular `they` only where rewording fails (`U18`), and title
  case that capitalizes words of four or more letters (`U23`).
- **APA style.** `who` for people (`U12`) and the same four-letter rule for title
  case (`U23`).

## Openly licensed guides

These contributed conventions rather than prose. Their licenses permit reuse; two of
them require attribution, which `NOTICE` gives.

- **Federal Plain Language Guidelines**, by the Plain Language Action and
  Information Network (PLAIN), published by GSA at plainlanguage.gov until 2025.
  Hidden verbs (`C2`–`C4`); subject, verb, and object kept close together (`C15`);
  and one term for one thing (`C22`). A US Government work: public domain in the
  United States, with a worldwide CC0 1.0 waiver, so no attribution is required.
  The site now redirects to Digital.gov; its pages survive in the archived
  `GSA/plainlanguage.gov` repository on GitHub.
- **18F Content Guide**, GSA. Sentence case for headings and page titles alike
  (`U23`), one through nine spelled out (`U19`), dates with the month spelled out
  (`U22`), capitals only where a name needs them (`U24`), and inclusive wording
  (`U18`). Public domain in the United States, with a worldwide CC0 1.0 waiver, so
  no attribution is required. The guide survives in the archived
  `18F/content-guide` repository on GitHub.
- **Google developer documentation style guide.** Licensed CC BY 4.0. Latin
  abbreviations (`U15`), inclusive wording (`U18`), and the conventions in
  `U19`–`U28`, among them sentence-case headings (`U23`) and the hyphen it uses for
  ranges (`U21`).
- **GOV.UK style guide**, Government Digital Service. Open Government Licence v3.0.
  Words in place of `eg`, `etc`, and `ie` (`U15`), dates in day-month-year order
  (`U22`), sentence case even in page titles (`U23`), and specific link text
  (`U27`).

## Slop rules: observation of model output

`S1`–`S24` are the project's own catalog, written from reading model output. No
book or study supplied their content. Two outside sources support the premise and
the catalog:

- **Dmitry Kobak, Rita González-Márquez, Emőke-Ágnes Horvát, and Jan Lause,
  "Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess
  vocabulary," *Science Advances* 11, no. 27 (2025).** First posted to arXiv in June
  2024 as "Delving into ChatGPT usage in academic writing through excess
  vocabulary." Certain style words rose abruptly in PubMed abstracts once LLMs
  arrived, most of them verbs and adjectives, with `delves`, `showcasing`, and
  `underscores` among those that rose the most. The study supports the premise of
  `S1`, that models use some words far more often than people do.
- **Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing."** An advice page kept by WikiProject AI
  Cleanup, consulted in September 2026. It catalogs many of the same patterns:
  model-favored words (`S1`, `S2`), inflated statements of importance and trailing
  `-ing` clauses (`S13`), negative parallelisms (`S8`, `S9`), the rule of three
  (`S15`), inline-header lists (`S16`), heavy use of em dashes, emoji, and boldface
  (`S21`, `S22`, `S23`), and chatbot phrases addressed to the user (`S10`, `S12`). Like
  `slop.md`, it treats these as signs to weigh rather than as proof.

Prior art for the category is **stop-slop** by Hardik Pandya
(`hardikpandya/stop-slop`, MIT), a skill file for removing AI tells from prose. It
predates this corpus. The `S` rules share its subject and none of its text.

## Rules from the project's own reviews

`M1`–`M4` are original to this project. The corpus's first review of a production
site, in September 2026, made three confident mistakes: it blamed an edit for a
problem that predated the edit, reported a shift between table rows that only a
flattened copy showed, and called a coherent number style inconsistent. `M1`, `M2`,
and `M4` exist to prevent them. The same review found the project's own copy-review
agent without being asked and applied its em dash ban, which gave better findings
than any default would have; `M3` makes that step a rule.

`C23` came from the same work. A test page built to reproduce that review's trap
held two defects no rule covered: it promised five tools and listed six, and it
offered the plan "for 30 days" above a "Start free trial" button.

## Rules without a single source

`C12`, `C14`, `U3`, `U4`, `U7`, `U8`, and `U9` restate common editorial practice
that we did not trace to one source.

## Prior art in tooling

Worth knowing about, and worth using alongside this corpus:

- **proselint** (BSD): a prose linter whose modules encode advice from Garner,
  Pinker, Orwell, and others. Deterministic where this corpus is judgmental.
- **Vale** (MIT): a configurable prose linter with packages implementing the
  Microsoft and Google style guides.
- **write-good** and **alex** (MIT): narrow linters for weak constructions and for
  insensitive wording.

## What we are not

This corpus is not a fact checker, a plagiarism detector, or a full grammar checker.
It can tell when a document disagrees with itself (`C23`), not which version is
true. It has no opinion about whether a text was written by a machine, only about
whether it reads like one. Those are different questions. A human can write slop; a
model can write well.
